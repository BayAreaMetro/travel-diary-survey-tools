2 — Balancer Performance

Did the balancer converge and how well do the weighted totals match PUMS-derived targets?
What to look for:
• Convergence: Did the balancer converge within the specified tolerance and iteration limit?
• Household/Person: Population totals are derived from the first household- and person-level control. Do both household and persons match? If not you may need to apply greater importance on h_total or p_total to overcome the inherent sampling bias against large-households that results in total population being deflated.
• Mean Absolute Percent Error (MAPE) is the mean |% error| across all control categories for the zone. Average fit.
• P90 is the 90th percentile |% error|; highlights how bad the worst-fitting categories are without being swamped by a single outlier.
• Max is the single worst |% error| across all categories for the zone.

3 — Weight Quality

How variable or extreme are the resulting weights?
What to look for:
• Coefficient of Variation (CV): standard deviation / mean of the weights. High CV (e.g. above 0.5) indicates extreme weight variability, which can lead to unstable estimates.
• Effective Sample Size (ESS) = (∑w)2 / ∑w2 (Kish statistic); above 80% is good, below 50% suggests a few records dominate. Derived from the design effect in survey sampling, quantifying how much estimate variability is inflated due to weighting.
• Expansion factors (EF): the ratio of final weight to base weight. Min/Max EF hitting the configured bounds means those households were constrained and the balancer wanted to push them further.
• We expect a roughly log-normal distribution of weights and not too lumpy; it is plotted on log-scale so it should be closer to “normal”.
• Multi-modality is not wrong per se, but is indicative that weights are being driven by a few key variables (e.g. a single dominant control category) and may be unstable.

5 — Unweighted Cell Counts (Data Sparsity)

Unweighted household or person counts per control category per zone versus PUMS-derived targets in italic parentheses to provide context for how well-represented a category is in the underlying population.

What to look for:
• Low overall sample count in a zone is a candidate for merging that zone with another (e.g. merge two adjacent low-sample zones into one larger zone). Zones don't technically need to be adjacent to be merged, but it makes intuitive sense and usually aligns with larger contextual geography.
• Low counts across all zones are candidate for global category merging (e.g. collapsing age 65+ instead of 65-74, 75-84, 85+).
• Low counts in a specific zone-category pair can be surgically merged for that cell only. But be careful with this approach as it can lead to a patchwork of different category definitions across zones, which can be hard to track and interpret.
• There is no hard threshold for what constitutes a “low” count, but cells with fewer than 30 unweighted records are highlighted in red as a warning sign. The appropriate threshold can depend on the context and the variability of the data, but 10 is a common rule of thumb in survey analysis for minimum cell counts to ensure stability of estimates.
• If a category has a low sample count but also represents a small percentage of the population, it may be less concerning than a low count in a category that represents a large share of the population. If PUMS target is also low then it is a strong candidate for merging.
