1 — Profile Comparison

One row per balancing run. Every profile is fitted to the same controls over its own universe, so the household and person totals should land on the same targets — a difference here is the usability gate changing what the survey can reach, not the balancer failing.
What to look for:
• Two rows that match exactly mean two profiles admitted the same households and you are paying for two balancing runs to get one answer. Check whether the axes that separate them actually separate anything.
• Σ weights should agree across profiles to within a fraction of a percent, since all of them expand onto the same regional totals. A profile that lands well short is not reaching its targets.
• ESS% and max/med falling as the universe grows is normal — a larger universe spreads the same population over more records.

2 — Crosswalk Map

This map depicts the spatial crosswalk from PUMAs to the target geographies. This allows us to use any custom target geography, regardless of PUMA geography. If zones overlap, the PUMAs are allocated using a block-group-level population-weighted share of the PUMA that contributes to that target geography. If PUMAs nest within the target geographies, then the allocations are all 100% or 0%.
The geometry belongs to the run rather than to any one fit, so it is drawn once and each zone's tooltip lists every profile's seed count. Boundaries are simplified to a few metres — imperceptible at this zoom, and the difference between a report that opens instantly and one that does not.

3 — Weight Cascade

What carries weight below the household. Every profile is shown at once: these counts are small enough to compare directly, and the household-level answer alone says nothing about what reached persons, days and trips.
Each level's rows split three ways, exhaustively:
• Usable — admitted by this profile's own flag, read off that level's own row.
• Weighted — admitted and carrying a non-zero weight.
• Unweighted — admitted and carrying none. Below the household this means the record's household was dropped, so a person can be flagged usable and still carry nothing. That is the cascade working, but anyone filtering on the flag and expecting a weight needs to see it. At the household level it means the control geography could not place the household — see the coverage table below.

{{ cascade_table }}

Redistribution carried by the survivors

A record's weight over its parent's, within admitted parents. Unusable records carry no weight, but their population does not vanish — it is carried by the usable records in the same scope, every one of them scaled by the same factor. So this ratio is that factor: 1.0 where every sibling was usable, and rising as the survivors stand in for more of them.
A maximum of 10 means one person representing a ten-person household. Nothing is wrong with that arithmetically, but it is the largest single lever on bias in the procedure, and it is worth knowing how much of the sample sits out in that tail.

{{ redistribution_table }}

Day split identity

Days divide their person's weight instead of copying it: a person's usable days jointly represent that person once, so they must sum back to the person weight. That is arithmetic the pipeline controls, so a residual beyond floating-point noise is a bug rather than a finding — it is checked here rather than assumed.
Stranded counts parents carrying weight with no usable child at all. Their weight is deliberately left unrepresented below rather than pooled onto other parents, which would silently distort per-day totals while still passing a household-level checksum.

{{ split_identity_table }}

Control geography coverage

A household outside every control polygon belongs to no balancing zone, so no fit can give it a weight. That is a bound on what the weighting can answer rather than a defect in the household, and it is stated here rather than inferred from a column of nulls.

4 — Fractional Seed Imputation — {{ pane.profile }}

This is not the survey imputation step, and nothing here changes survey data. That step makes a discrete decision — it picks a value for a missing field and writes it into the canonical tables, where every downstream consumer reads it. What is reported below happens only inside this weighting run, on the seed incidence matrix, and is discarded once the weights are fitted. The control totals are never touched: they come from PUMS and stay fixed.

A household with a null control value contributes an all-zero row to that control's incidence block — it still counts in h_total, but it silently drops out of that one constraint, as if it had been deleted from the gender table alone. Rather than let that happen, those zeros are replaced with class-membership probabilities from a Random Forest trained on complete PUMS microdata: a record becomes, say, 0.6 male and 0.4 female. The balancer accepts non-negative floats, so a fractional row is valid input (fractional imputation, Kim & Fuller 2004). It is a last resort that keeps a record in the constraint, not a statement about what that household actually is.

Read the table as how much of the seed leaned on this, and how trustworthy the lean was. Controls exceeding 25% null are flagged — consider removing them as targets if the RF quality is also poor, since at that point the constraint is being met largely by predictions.
• Null %: share of seed rows whose incidence block for this control was empty.
• log_loss: Cross-entropy between predicted probabilities and true labels, cross-validated on the PUMS training data (lower is better; < 0.5 is good).
• F1: Macro-averaged F1 score across all categories (higher is better; > 0.7 is good).
• A dash means no model was fitted — the control had no nulls, or a category had too few training records to cross-validate.

5 — Balancer Performance — {{ pane.profile }}

Did the balancer converge and how well do the weighted totals match PUMS-derived targets?
What to look for:
• Convergence: Did the balancer converge within the specified tolerance and iteration limit?
• Household/Person: Population totals are derived from the first household- and person-level control. Do both household and persons match? If not you may need to apply greater importance on h_total or p_total to overcome the inherent sampling bias against large-households that results in total population being deflated.
• Mean Absolute Percent Error (MAPE) is the mean |% error| across all control categories for the zone. Average fit.
• P90 is the 90th percentile |% error|; highlights how bad the worst-fitting categories are without being swamped by a single outlier.
• Max is the single worst |% error| across all categories for the zone.
• Coefficient of Variation (CV): standard deviation / mean of the weights. CV depends heavily on the number and type of controls:
  • < 0.6 — excellent; typical only with few (2–3) household-level controls
  • 0.6–1.0 — good; expected with a moderate control spec (4–6 controls)
  • 1.0–1.5 — typical for rich control specs with many person-level controls aggregated to households (e.g. age, commute mode, employment, gender, student status); not inherently problematic if fit is acceptable
  • > 1.5 — high; review whether controls are competing or cells are too sparse
• Effective Sample Size (ESS) = (∑w)2 / ∑w2 (Kish statistic), expressed as a percentage of the unweighted sample size. ESS is inversely related to the design effect (DEFF = 1/ESS%). Expected values depend on the control specification complexity:
  • > 60% — excellent; generally only achievable with few controls or very large samples
  • 40–60% — good; typical for travel diary surveys balancing 6–10 controls including person-level variables
  • 30–40% — acceptable; common when many person-level controls create high-dimensional household incidence vectors
  • < 30% — low; a few records likely dominate — investigate merges, drop competing controls, or relax expansion bounds
Person-level controls (age, gender, employment, commute mode, etc.) are each aggregated to household counts, creating high-dimensional incidence vectors. Each additional person-level control can substantially reduce ESS because household-level weights must simultaneously satisfy many per-person constraints. A rich spec with 5+ person-level controls will typically land in the 35–55% range.

6 — Weight Quality — {{ pane.profile }}

How variable or extreme are the resulting weights?
We expect a roughly log-normal distribution of weights and not too lumpy; it is plotted on log-scale so it should be closer to “normal”. Multi-modality is not wrong per se, but is indicative that weights are being driven by a few key variables (e.g. a single dominant control category) and may be unstable. What to look for:
• Expansion factors (EF): the ratio of final weight to base weight. Min/Max EF hitting the configured bounds means those households were constrained and the balancer wanted to push them further. This means the data are being spread too thin and some broader aggregation is required.

{% if pane.ef_tradeoff_section %}9{% else %}8{% endif %} — Unweighted Cell Counts (Data Sparsity) — {{ pane.profile }}

Unweighted household or person counts per control category per zone versus PUMS-derived targets in italic parentheses to provide context for how well-represented a category is in the underlying population.

What to look for:
• Low overall sample count in a zone is a candidate for merging that zone with another (e.g. merge two adjacent low-sample zones into one larger zone). Zones don't technically need to be adjacent to be merged, but it makes intuitive sense and usually aligns with larger contextual geography.
• Low counts across all zones are candidate for global category merging (e.g. collapsing age 65+ instead of 65-74, 75-84, 85+).
• Low counts in a specific zone-category pair can be surgically merged for that cell only. But be careful with this approach as it can lead to a patchwork of different category definitions across zones, which can be hard to track and interpret.
• There is no hard threshold for what constitutes a “low” count, but cells with fewer than 30 unweighted records are highlighted in red as a warning sign. The appropriate threshold can depend on the context and the variability of the data, but 10 is a common rule of thumb in survey analysis for minimum cell counts to ensure stability of estimates.
• If a category has a low sample count but also represents a small percentage of the population, it may be less concerning than a low count in a category that represents a large share of the population. If PUMS target is also low then it is a strong candidate for merging.
